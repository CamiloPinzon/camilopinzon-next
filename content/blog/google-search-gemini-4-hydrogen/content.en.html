What happened

On November 12, 2026, Google announced that its search engine now runs on Gemini 4 Hydrogen, its new AI model, for every user in every country. Robby Stein, VP of Product for Google Search, calls it the biggest model upgrade Search has had.

The same day, Google published a new guide on how to spot visits from Google Search in your own analytics. The two go together, so we're covering them in one post.

What it means

An AI model is the "brain" that reads what you type and puts the answer together. Google swapped that brain in its search engine, and according to the announcement you'll notice the difference in a few ways:

  • It handles long questions, multi-part questions and typos better.
  • To answer, it runs several searches at once on the subtopics of your question. Google calls this "query fan-out." Think of an assistant planning a trip who checks flights, hotels and the weather at the same time instead of one after the other.
  • It shows more images, comparison tables and structured answers.
  • It gives more nuanced answers in every language that Search's AI mode supports.

Google says those parallel searches let Search point people to more links than before, including sites they might not have found otherwise.

How it affects you

The upside. If your site answers one specific piece of a question well (a price, opening hours, a comparison, a very particular case), it has a better shot at showing up, because Search now looks things up piece by piece. Google says outright that it wants to send people to new sites.

The risk. Google's answers now pack more information into the results page itself: tables, images, comparisons. That means more people can find what they were looking for without visiting any website. Say someone asks how much a service costs, Google shows them prices in a table, and they no longer need to open your page.

How many visits does this change win or lose? Google didn't say. Right now nobody knows whether your site will get more or fewer visitors, so if someone quotes you a percentage this week, be skeptical. There's no official data.

Who should care most. Any business that gets customers from Google, but especially sites that answer questions: guides, comparisons, "how much does it cost," "how do I." If most people find you by searching for your name or brand, the effect should be smaller.

Our take: don't rewrite your site because of this announcement. First make sure you're measuring properly. That part you can do today.

What you can do

  1. Mark the date. Add November 12 as a note in your analytics tool (Google Analytics supports annotations). That way you can compare before and after without guessing.
  2. Check two sources. Search Console counts the clicks people make on Google. Your analytics counts visits that actually loaded your page. Google explains that the two almost never match: ad blockers, private browsing or declining cookies keep some visits from being recorded, and Search Console leaves out ad clicks. Look at whether both go up or down together, not at the exact numbers.
  3. Make sure Google shows up as organic traffic. When someone clicks a result, the browser tells your site where they came from, a bit like the return address on an envelope. That signal is called the "Referer." According to Google, most analytics tools recognize it on their own and label it as organic search. If your reports show Google visits as "direct" or "referral," ask your team to check custom channel rules, referral exclusions and filters.
  4. Ask about redirects. Google warns that redirects done with JavaScript can wipe out that signal and make a visit from Google look like it came from your own site. If your site redirects pages that way, it's worth knowing before you draw conclusions from the data.

One thing that often trips people up: that signal tells you the visitor came from Google, not what they searched for. To see the searches that bring you visitors, use the Performance report in Search Console.
